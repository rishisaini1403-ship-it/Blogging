The Cipher That Killed a Queen: What Simon Singh's The Code Book Taught Me About Secrets

The dedication at the start of The Code Book isn't in English. It reads:

"To the Teachers and Mortals who took the time to inspire me XICYIQKMHR, VOIR RFH LKRQT"

At first glance it looks like nonsense — maybe a typo, maybe a different language entirely. But it isn't either. It's a message, deliberately scrambled, waiting for someone with the right key to unlock it. Which, it turns out, is the entire point of the book.

Simon Singh's The Code Book tells the story of cryptography: the centuries-long arms race between people trying to keep secrets and people trying to steal them. Kings, spies, mathematicians, lovers, generals — for as long as people have had something to hide, someone else has been trying to read it anyway. And as Singh points out, no code has ever stayed unbreakable forever. Every clever cipher eventually meets someone clever enough to crack it. He compares it to doctors and bacteria: you invent a stronger antibiotic, the bacteria evolve resistance, you invent a stronger antibiotic again. Codemakers and codebreakers have been locked in that same cycle since antiquity — and they still are, every time you send a text message or check your bank balance online.

Nowhere in the book is that struggle more dramatic than in the opening chapter, which tells the true story of a queen who trusted her cipher with her life — and lost.

A Queen, a Barrel of Beer, and a Fatal Mistake

In 1586, Mary, Queen of Scots was a prisoner in England, and a problem for Queen Elizabeth I. Catholic conspirators wanted Mary on the English throne instead, and Mary — desperate and isolated — began corresponding with them in secret. Her letters went in and out of her prison at Chartley Hall hidden inside beer barrels, encrypted using a nomenclator: a hybrid cipher that swapped letters for symbols and slipped in extra code-words for good measure. Mary believed, completely, that no one else could read them.

She was wrong.

Her courier, Gilbert Gifford, was a double agent working for Sir Francis Walsingham, Elizabeth's spymaster. Every letter Mary sent was secretly copied and handed to Thomas Phelippes, England's best codebreaker, before it ever reached its destination. When Anthony Babington wrote to Mary laying out a full plot to assassinate Elizabeth and free her, Mary made the fatal error of writing back with her approval — encrypted, she thought safely, in her own cipher.

Phelippes broke it easily. And then, not content with simply reading the evidence, he forged an extra postscript onto Mary's own letter, asking Babington to name every conspirator involved. Babington did. Walsingham rounded up the entire network. Mary was tried, and her deciphered letters were used as the proof that convicted her. On 8 February 1587, she was executed.

The cipher she trusted to protect her became the very thing that destroyed her.

What Actually Struck Me

Before reading this, I associated encryption with computers — passwords, HTTPS, two-factor authentication. It hadn't occurred to me that centuries before any of that existed, the strength of a cipher could decide whether a monarch lived or died.

But the part that's stayed with me isn't really about the cryptography at all. It's that every major turn in Mary's story was driven by very human things: trust, ambition, betrayal, patience, overconfidence. The cipher itself was just a tool. The real story is about how people used it, misused it, and — fatally, in Mary's case — trusted it too much. Technology doesn't change history by itself. People do. Technology just amplifies whatever they were already going to do.

That's the same story playing out today between cybersecurity teams and hackers, just with better math.

And that strange dedication at the start of the book? By the time I finished the first chapter, I understood it wasn't meaningless at all. It was never hidden — it was just waiting. Nothing looks like a secret because it's confusing. It's a secret because only someone with the right key can see what was there all along.
